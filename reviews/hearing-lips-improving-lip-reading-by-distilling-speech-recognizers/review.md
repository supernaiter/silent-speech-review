# Hearing Lips: Improving Lip Reading by Distilling Speech Recognizers

Zhao, Y., Xu, R., Wang, X., Hou, P., Tang, H., Song, M. · AAAI 2020 · 2020 · [doi:10.1609/aaai.v34i04.6174](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v34i04.6174) · [arXiv:1911.11502](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.11502)

## Verdict

The paper [[1]](#ref-1) changes the training-only supervision (M02): a speech recognizer is distilled into a sentence-level lip reader at three granularities, transcripts kept. Before 2019-05, audio entered sentence-level lip-reader training as forced-alignment targets (13 of 77 decoders) and never as distilled teacher outputs (0 of 77, pattern match, Appendix), so the design is ahead at the time, shared with the [concurrent] Afouras et al. [[2]](#ref-2). The gain repeats on CMLR and LRS2 and with all three teachers, each a single value without a run count (§4), but the rationale that the benefit comes from audio is untested: a [later] same-student comparison finds a video teacher at least as useful [[3]](#ref-3). The state-of-the-art claim (M12) holds for CMLR only; on LRS2 the paper's own Table 5 lists lower WER for two earlier systems.

## Against the field, component by component

### M02 Training-only supervision: recognizer teacher distilled into the lip reader

**Usual approach:** In 77 sentence-level lip-reading and audio-visual decoders first public before 2019-05, supervision is the transcript; 13 also take hard forced-alignment labels from an audio recognizer as targets for a visual or audio-visual model (pattern match, Appendix), e.g. [[4]](#ref-4), [[5]](#ref-5); none of the 77 distils a recognizer's outputs or states (Appendix). LIBS replaces hard labels with soft teacher targets.

**State of the art:** Ren et al. (2021) [[3]](#ref-3) [later] isolate the step on LRS2: one student gives 57.5% WER without distillation, 54.2% with an audio teacher and 53.4% with a video teacher (Table 1). That audio-teacher gain, 3.3 points (5.7% relative), is close to LIBS's 2.90 WER points on LRS2 (68.19% to 65.29%, 4.3% relative) from a baseline about 11 points weaker (inference).

Whole-wiki recall finds no study before 2019-05 distilling an audio teacher into a non-acoustic decoder (Appendix). The work Afouras et al. call "the closest work to ours", Li (Wei) et al. [[6]](#ref-6), distils into an audio-visual student, not lip reading "from the video signal alone" (as described in [[2]](#ref-2), §1.1). [concurrent]: Afouras et al. report 58.5% against 57.9% LRS2 WER for ground-truth CTC against distillation then ground-truth fine-tuning [[2]](#ref-2); the throat-microphone student of [[7]](#ref-7) is acoustic.

The effect repeats: CER falls from 38.93% to 31.27% on CMLR and from 48.28% to 45.53% on LRS2 (Table 3), 19.7% and 5.7% relative (inference); every teacher beats WAS on CMLR (31.27% to 34.94% against 38.93%, Table 2); the CMLR margin grows to 9.63 points with 20% of the data (Table 4). Verdict: ahead, on design absence plus repetition over datasets and teachers.

### M12 Evaluation: state-of-the-art claim and run counts

**Usual approach:** None of the 4 earlier decoders reporting visual-only LRS2 error rates states seeds or repeated runs (pattern match, hits read, Appendix); LIBS also gives one value per row (Tables 2–5).

**State of the art:** USR (2024) [[8]](#ref-8) [later] reaches 16.0% LRS2 visual-only WER without an LM, with 1,759 unlabelled hours (Table 6); trained on CMLR alone, Ma et al. (2022) [[9]](#ref-9) [later] report 9.1% CER, mean of ten runs. LIBS is 49.3 and 22.2 points higher (inference).

The abstract claims "the new state-of-the-art performance on the CMLR and LRS2 datasets". Table 5 supports CMLR (31.27% against 32.48% for the authors' own [concurrent] CSSMCM [[10]](#ref-10)); on LRS2 it lists CTC/Attention [[11]](#ref-11) at 42.1% CER and 63.5% WER and TM-seq2seq at 49.8% WER, against 45.53% and 65.29%. The authors concede TM-seq2seq "achieves the lowest WER on the LRS2 dataset" (§4). CTC/Attention decodes with an external LM [[11]](#ref-11); LIBS does not state its LM use. TM-seq2seq's 49.8% is its no-LM row [[12]](#ref-12). Verdict: standard, as the field also gave no run counts.

## Where the claims outrun the evidence

- The motivating 10.4% against 39.5% CER, "Given the same amount of training data and model architecture" (§1), comes from Chung et al. [[13]](#ref-13) on LRS, not LRS2, where the audio model also trains on an auxiliary audio-only set of 342,644 utterances (Tables 4–5).
- The claim that audio gives "complementary and discriminant clues" (Abstract) lacks a same-modality teacher control; Ren et al. [[3]](#ref-3) and lip-reader self-distillation [[14]](#ref-14), both [later], show video teachers help too.
- Teacher 2 has lower ASR CER than Teacher 1 (9.23% against 9.83%) but a worse student (34.94% against 31.27%, Table 2); the authors' explanation, mismatched feature dimensions (§4), is the same change as a wider teacher.
- Frame-level distillation adds 0.44 CER points over sequence plus context on LRS2 (45.97% to 45.53%, Table 3), a single value (inference).
- CMLR is split at random 7:1:2 over eleven hosts [[10]](#ref-10), so its test speakers are seen in training (inference); §4 does not say so.

## What the field needs next

- M02: one student distilled from audio, video and audio-visual teachers of matched size, several seeds, on LRS2 and CMLR.
- M02: per-granularity and LCS-filter ablations with repeated runs.
- M12: comparators decoded under one LM setting; a speaker-disjoint CMLR test.
- M03: teachers trained on training-partition audio only.

## Questions for the authors

- Is an external language model used in decoding?
- Which split selected the grid-searched λ weights and checkpoints?
- Did teacher training include CMLR or LRS2 test audio?
- How many runs underlie each row of Tables 2–4?
