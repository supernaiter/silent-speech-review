# Learning from the Master: Distilling Cross-modal Advanced Knowledge for Lip Reading

Ren, S., Du, Y., Lv, J., Han, G., He, S. · CVPR 2021 · 2021 · [doi:10.1109/CVPR46437.2021.01312](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR46437.2021.01312)

## Verdict

The paper [[1]](#ref-1) changes the teacher (M02), a trainable audio-visual "master" updated from a temporary student, and the sample order (M03), a tutor-confidence curriculum. Before 2020-12, none of 221 lip-reading decoders updates a teacher from student feedback (pattern match, Appendix); the design is new, but its isolating comparison is one LRS2 value per row, so it stays standard. The curriculum is standard: earlier decoders already order by length, contrary to §3.4, and the gain falls to 0.1 WER points on the strongest backend (inference), as the authors concede. The strongest confound: a same-structure video teacher alone gives about half the gain of the best master setting (57.5% to 53.4% WER against 49.6%, Tables 1 and 5). The state-of-the-art claim (M12) holds only against no-LM rows [[2]](#ref-2) [[3]](#ref-3) [[4]](#ref-4).

## Against the field, component by component

### M02 Training-only supervision: trainable audio-visual master

**Usual approach:** Of 221 lip-reading, VSR and AVSR decoders before 2020-12, 4 distil (pattern match, Appendix): LIBS [[5]](#ref-5) and Afouras et al. [[6]](#ref-6) from a pretrained recognizer, born-again distillation from a same-architecture video model [[4]](#ref-4), and two video branches trained jointly [[7]](#ref-7). None of the 221 uses an audio-visual teacher or updates the teacher from the student (Appendix); the master does both (§3.3).

**State of the art:** SyncVSR (2024) [[8]](#ref-8) [later] reaches 93.2% LRW top-1 without word boundaries, mean of three runs, LRW only (157 h), with audio tokens as targets. The paper's best LRW-only figure, 11.8% WER (Table 6), is 88.2% accuracy, 5.0 points lower (inference).

The authors contrast online distillation, which ignores "the feedback of each student" (§2); Afouras et al. name a fixed-teacher ASR-to-AVSR study [[9]](#ref-9) as closest. None of 21 [concurrent] decoders updates a teacher from feedback (Appendix). In Table 4 a master beats its fixed counterpart in every modality: 54.1% to 51.5% WER audio-visual, 54.2% to 52.1% audio, 53.4% to 53.0% video, 4.8%, 3.9% and 0.7% relative (inference), though each master is the weaker recognizer (§4.4). Verdict: standard; ahead needs repetition across seeds or datasets, and these are single LRS2 values.

### M03 Training: tutor-confidence curriculum

**Usual approach:** 9 of 221 prior decoders use a curriculum and 6 state a length order, words or short sentences first [[10]](#ref-10) [[2]](#ref-2) [[11]](#ref-11); 0 order by difficulty or confidence (pattern match, Appendix). The paper ranks samples by tutor confidence with exponential pacing (§3.4).

**State of the art:** No read lip-reading study isolates a difficulty order; the [concurrent] sub-word reader (2021) [[12]](#ref-12) drops length curricula as needing "a complicated tuning process" (§3). The paper's own gain is 1.1, 0.5 and 0.1 WER points over three backends (Table 6), 5.6%, 3.6% and 0.8% relative (inference).

§3.4 says [1, 7, 27] "randomly sample training data"; all three use a curriculum, two stating single words first [[2]](#ref-2) [[11]](#ref-11). Only the ordering criterion is new (difficulty order 0 of 221, Appendix). The authors concede that with an advanced backend CL "mainly helps to accelerate the training" (§4.4). Verdict: standard; one run per row, no length-ordered control.

### M12 Evaluation: state-of-the-art claim

**Usual approach:** Of 17 prior decoders mentioning LRS2, 11 mention no run spread and 13 a language model (mention counts, Appendix). The paper gives one WER per row and no LM statement.

**State of the art:** USR (2024) [[13]](#ref-13) [later] reaches 16.0% LRS2 WER without an LM (223 labelled, 1,759 unlabelled hours); USR 2.0 (2026) [[14]](#ref-14) [later] 17.6% on LRS3. The paper's 49.2% and 59.0% are 33.2 and 41.4 points higher (inference).

Table 2's copied values match their sources but not their conditions. TM-Seq2Seq's 49.8% and 59.9%, with MV-LRS data as the authors note (§4.3), are no-LM rows; with an LM it gives 48.3% and 58.9% [[2]](#ref-2), below the paper. TM-CTC-KD's 51.3% and 59.8% use a 6-gram LM and extra data [[6]](#ref-6). V2P [[3]](#ref-3) reports 55.1 ± 0.9% on LRS3 (3,886 h, non-public); its ±0.9 error equals the paper's LRS3 margin over TM-Seq2Seq (inference). On LRW, Table 2 omits Martinez et al. [[15]](#ref-15), listed at 14.7% in Table 6 against Ours-TM's 14.3%, and born-again distillation at 87.9% top-1 [[4]](#ref-4). Verdict: standard.

## Where the claims outrun the evidence

- The 6.7-point LRW gain over "its baseline Temporal Convolution" (§4.3) uses a copied 25.4%; the paper's own 2-layer baseline is 24.3% (Table 6), a 5.6-point gain (inference).
- A video teacher helps more than an audio teacher (53.4% against 54.2%, Table 1), as the authors acknowledge; the video master adds 0.4 points (inference).
- Master against teacher also changes access to the validation split (§3.3).

## What the field needs next

- M02: fixed audio-visual teacher, master without feedback and full master on LRW and LRS2, several seeds, validation data held out.
- M03: confidence, length and random order at equal training hours on sentences.
- M12: comparators at one LM setting, with repeated runs.

## Questions for the authors

- Is an external LM or beam search used on LRS2 and LRS3?
- Which validation split feeds the master, and does it also select checkpoints?
- How many runs underlie Tables 4–6?
- Why does Table 2 leave out Martinez et al., whose backend Table 6 uses?
